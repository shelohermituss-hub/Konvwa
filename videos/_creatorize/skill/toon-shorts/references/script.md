# Writing the narration

The narration drives everything: every clause becomes a picture, every stressed word lands a face, a move or a prop
event. Write it so it can be acted out literally, fast and dry.

## 1. Shape and numbers

| | target | range |
|---|---|---|
| length | 35-45 s | 30-70 s |
| speed | ~215 words a minute (~3.6 words a second) | 205-240 |
| words | 125-160 for 35-45 s | |
| sentences | 9-13 | up to ~23 in a long one |
| words per sentence | ~14 | 9-16 |
| a new picture beat | every ~1.1-1.2 s = about every 4 words | never more than 1.5 s without one |

The voice runs wall to wall: pauses of ~0.2 s after a sentence, ~0.18 s at a comma, none over 0.3 s (the voice tool
tightens the recording to this). So write sentences that flow into each other; a pause is not a dramatic device here,
a picture is. One 0.5 s beat per short is allowed, filled with a sound effect.

## 2. Second person: "you" are the hero

- Speak to the viewer and make them the character: "You press the button. Nothing happens. You press it again."
- Every "you" line is acted by the hero on screen. Other people are "he", "she", "the guy", a job title.
- The hero never speaks; the narrator does. If someone on screen says a quoted line, keep it to one short line.

## 3. The hook (first sentence, first 2 seconds)

- Start mid-situation, never with an intro ("In this video...", "Have you ever wondered...").
- Good hooks (shapes, with made-up examples): a question with a stake ("Where does the water go when you flush on a
  plane?"); a rule and the thing that breaks it ("Every elevator has a door-close button. Most of them do nothing.");
  the outcome shown first ("You just won a free cruise."); a promise of harm ("Eat one of these berries and you won't
  feel your tongue until Tuesday.").
- Don't reuse a hook, joke or line you have seen in someone else's video. Same shape, new content.
- The picture pays the hook off within 2-3 s: the event happens, the gag lands, the stake is visible. Write the first
  two sentences so that payoff is possible (a concrete action, not an abstraction).

## 4. One clause = one picture

- Split every sentence into clauses of ~4-8 words. Each clause gets its own picture: a new shot when it starts a new
  sentence, otherwise a new beat inside the running shot (a move, a face swap, a prop event).
- So each clause must be **showable literally**: a concrete subject, a concrete verb, a concrete object. "The salt
  pulls the water out" (water droplets with worried faces sliding out of a cucumber slice) works; "the process is
  complex" does not.
- Put the word that matters (the noun, the verb, the number) where it can be stressed: the engine lands faces, props
  and camera arrivals on stressed words.
- Short punch clauses (2-3 words: "It doesn't.", "Not even close.") are great: they get a face swap or a punch-in.

## 5. Analogies made literal

- The style's signature: explain a mechanism by building an analogy as a real place or object, then acting in it.
  Typical shapes: a system becomes a building whose rooms are its parts; a signal becomes a cord or a lamp that goes
  on and off; a hidden rule becomes a document inside the machine; a distribution becomes a row of buildings of
  different heights; a level or state becomes a floor, a depth or a dial. Pick a pairing of your own for each topic.
- Say the analogy plainly ("Picture your battery as a parking garage.") and then keep talking INSIDE it ("Every charge
  is a car looking for a spot..."). Don't keep switching back to the literal terms; return once, at the twist.
- One big analogy per short (two at most), plus small visual puns on single words.

## 6. Numbers

- Use few numbers (3-6 a short), each concrete and spoken in full where it can land on an object: "three hundred
  kilos", "forty floors", "one in fifty", "eight minutes".
- Every spoken number gets a physical event on its word (a counter landing, a button lighting, an object placed), so
  give each number its own clause, not a list of five in one breath. Ranges read well as runs ("from ten all the way up
  to ninety").

## 7. Tone

- Dry, confident, a little mischievous. Ironic lines followed by a deadpan reaction beat (the hero's unimpressed face
  holds ~1 s).
- Contrast between words and picture is a joke tool: the line says "terrified", the face is bored, then it snaps.
- No hedging, no filler ("basically", "actually" only as a joke), no lists of facts without an image.

## 8. Structure (pick one)

1. **Journey:** question -> follow the thing through the system, step by step -> the apparent answer -> "but how?" ->
   the analogy built as a place -> the twist -> back to the start.
2. **Story + analogy:** a small story (a crime, a trick, a mistake) -> the analogy that explains it -> a second idea
   -> back to the story with the proof -> the verdict.
3. **Follow:** follow someone through a world with strange rules until the loophole is revealed.
4. **Survival / consequence:** "you" in one place over a story clock (day, night, next day); each mistake acted out,
   then explained with an insert (x-ray, map, cross-section).

## 9. Endings

- **Default: a loop.** End where you started (the same place, the same state) and write the last line so it runs into
  the first ("...and the last 20 % takes the rest of the night." -> "Your phone is at 1 %..."). The last word ends
  ~0.25 s before the video does; nothing after it but a tiny button (a blink, a nod, an object rolling in).
- Or a **bookend**: the opening prop or situation returns, flipped.
- Or a **verdict tableau**: the punchline written on a prop (a ledger, a sign, a report card) and one deadpan face.
- Never: "subscribe", "let me know in the comments", a summary of what was said.

## 10. Before you record: checklist

- [ ] First sentence is a hook that a picture can pay off within 2-3 s.
- [ ] Every clause names something you can show (no abstract clause without an analogy object).
- [ ] "You" is the hero throughout.
- [ ] 3-6 numbers, each in its own clause, said in full.
- [ ] One analogy built as a place, said plainly, then lived in.
- [ ] At least two dry lines that earn a deadpan reaction.
- [ ] The last line loops into the first (or bookends it).
- [ ] 125-160 words for 35-45 s at ~215 wpm.

## Example (original topic, annotated)

"Your phone is at 1 % and you plug it in. (hook: an ECU of a red battery icon, a cable slides in) | For the first
twenty minutes, it charges like crazy. (the battery fills fast, a counter races) | Then it crawls. (the counter slows to
a drip, deadpan face) | Picture your battery as a parking garage. (pull out: the phone becomes a garage) | Each charge
is a car looking for a spot. (cars stream in) | When the garage is empty, they park anywhere. (cars dart into spaces) |
But near the top floors, every car has to search. (one car circles, the driver squints) | So your phone slows down on
purpose, to stop a pile-up. (a garage attendant waves cars in one by one) | That's why 80 % takes half an hour... (a
sign: FLOOR 80) | and the last 20 % takes the rest of the night. (night falls, the hero asleep, the phone at 1 % again:
loop)"
